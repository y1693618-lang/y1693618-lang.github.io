The story

Telling the player without sound: vibration and text

How a game with no audio at all hands over the feel and the alerts that sound would normally carry, using the iPhone’s vibration and on-screen text. What the author hesitated over, what was hardest, and what only became clear afterwards.

Crystal Cavern is an idle game that uses no sound at all. Why I decided against sound is in Why no sound. The visual feel of a tap, the crystal swelling and the numbers floating up, and the work on colour and contrast are in Making of.

This page covers the two things those pages leave out: the vibration in the iPhone app, and the text of the game’s messages.

What does sound actually do in a game?

Once I had decided on no sound, the first question was what sound is really doing in an ordinary game. Written down, it came to five jobs. Crystal Cavern has a replacement for each one.

What sound doesWhat Crystal Cavern does instead
Feedbackthe click when you press somethingThe crystal swells, a number floats up, a light vibration
Marking a milestonethe fanfare when you level upA large on-screen message and a heavy vibration
Catching your attentionthe chime when a bonus appearsA glowing orb that drifts across the screen
Reaching you when you are awaythe notification soundNotifications that make no sound
Atmospheremusic that changes from place to placeCrystal and screen colours that change with each seam

Vibration was not my idea

To be honest, using vibration in place of sound was not my idea. It came up while I was talking the design through with an AI.

At first I wondered whether it would be all right. I worried that vibration that was too strong or too frequent would feel noisy in its own way. But when I actually tried it on my iPhone, it was just the right amount, and it felt good. Even without hearing anything, your fingertip gets a sense that you pressed something.

The strength of the vibration follows the size of the event.

EventVibration
Tapping the crystalThe lightest. It happens most often, so there is a short gap between pulses and fast tapping never turns into a constant buzz
Buying a buildingA little stronger
Catching a glowing orbA good moment, so it uses the “success” vibration
Reaching a new seamHeavy. A real milestone
RebirthThe “success” vibration followed by a heavy one. The biggest milestone of all

One rule was fixed from the start: vibration is only ever an extra. Everything can be understood from the screen alone. The browser version has no vibration at all and plays the same way. If “System Haptics” is turned off under Settings → Sounds & Haptics on the iPhone, the game does not vibrate either.

The hardest part was the text

The hardest thing to get right was the text of the messages. A sound reaches you in an instant, even if you are not looking at the screen. Text only reaches you if you look at it and read it. So the goal became readability of one hundred percent: if you look, you can always read it. In practice that meant:

  • Leave it on screen long enough to read. Large messages, such as reaching a new seam, stay for about 3.4 seconds. Shorter and they cannot be read; longer and they get in the way
  • Never stack them. If two things happen at once, the messages still appear one at a time, each waiting for the one before to leave. Overlapping messages are two messages nobody can read
  • Keep the important number on one line. When the crystal count grows very large, it switches to a form like “1.23 × 1041”. A number that wraps over several lines is hard to read and pushes the buttons out of reach
  • Make it readable in every colour scheme and every text size. There are four text sizes and five colour schemes. Which to choose is covered in Text size and colours
  • Mark things by shape as well as colour. For example, the small mark that appears on a tab when something there is ready is a ringed dot, so it can be told apart by its shape even if the colour is hard to see
  • Missing a message costs nothing. If you miss one, your crystals keep building up. If you miss a glowing orb, another comes along a little later

No sound means your ears are safe

Making everything readable on screen had a benefit I had not expected: it keeps your ears safe.

Because everything can be understood just by looking, there is no need to turn the volume up or to wear earphones, and no risk of damaging your hearing with loud sound. You can enjoy the game without taking on any risk of hearing loss. You can also play on a train or in the middle of the night without disturbing anyone.

A review I never expected

After the app reached the App Store, a player in China left a five-star review. In it was a line praising how light and just right the sound was.

Crystal Cavern has no sound at all. That review was something I had never expected. I do not know why it felt that way to them. Still, it was a happy surprise that a game with no sound could feel “just right”.

If you want to make a game without sound

To finish, here are five things I learned along the way.

  1. List what sound does, and choose a replacement for each job. It is easier to think of it as handing sound’s work to something else, rather than simply taking sound away
  2. Give each event both a visual and a vibration. If one is missed, the other still gets through
  3. Match the strength of the vibration to the size of the event. Make the most frequent action the lightest
  4. Check that text stays long enough to read, never overlaps, and fits on one line. Play the game yourself and confirm with your own eyes that you can read it
  5. Build it so that missing something costs nothing. Assuming people will miss things makes the game more relaxing for players and for the person making it

Having no sound means the game is exactly the same experience for people who can hear and for people who cannot. You can play Crystal Cavern in your browser straight from the home page.
